The Elements of Professionalism · Part Eight

The cost of a decision

We work in departments, which means most of you only ever see one part of what happens. This letter is about the part you cannot see from your own seat — where the money goes, who pays it, and why it matters more than almost anything else we do.

A departmental model is the right way to run this business. It is how we get specialists instead of generalists, and it is why our work is consistent.

It has one blind spot, and I want to close it.

When you make a decision in your seat, you usually see the part in front of you — the request, the resident, the deadline. What you often cannot see is where that decision lands: on an owner's statement, in an HOA letter, or on an invoice that arrives in someone else's inbox three weeks later.

Almost every decision we make has a dollar attached to it. Someone pays that dollar, and it is never nobody.

Why owners are so sensitive about expenses

It is worth understanding this properly, because it is easy to read an owner's reaction to a $185 invoice as them being difficult. Usually it is not.

Most of our owners are not wealthy institutions. They are people with one or two properties, and the economics are tighter than they look from outside. After the mortgage, taxes, insurance, HOA dues and maintenance, a great many of them clear very little each month — and in Florida, with insurance and taxes where they are, some clear nothing at all.

An unexpected $400 charge is not an annoyance to that owner. It is the month. Sometimes it is the quarter.

So when an invoice appears that they did not expect, did not approve, or do not understand, the reaction is not really about the money. It is about control — they have handed us their largest asset and they need to believe we are protecting it rather than spending it.

Every unexplained charge chips at that belief. Enough of them and the belief is gone, and with it the client.

How a small delay becomes a lost client

Here is the chain I want everyone to be able to see, because each link looks harmless on its own.

StepWhat happensHow it feels at the time
1An HOA violation notice arrivesRoutine. It goes in the queue.
2It sits a few days while other work gets doneReasonable. Everything is busy.
3The cure period expiresNobody notices.
4The association fines the ownerNow it is real, and it is their money.
5The owner pays a penalty caused by our delayThey are angry, and they are right to be.
6They start checking our workTrust is gone. Every statement gets scrutinised.
7They cancelOften months later, over something unrelated.

Not one of those steps required anyone to do anything wrong. It only required a letter to sit.

An HOA notice is not correspondence. It is a clock. The moment one arrives, someone's money is on a timer — and it is not ours.

Handle them the day they arrive. If the cure requires owner approval or a vendor, start that conversation immediately and tell the owner the deadline. An owner who knows about a deadline and chooses to miss it owns that outcome. An owner who finds out when the fine arrives blames us, and fairly.

Every time a vendor rolls, someone is paying

This is the one I most want to land, because it is where the largest avoidable costs come from.

A trip charge, a diagnostic fee, a dispatch for something that turned out not to be our responsibility — every one of those produces an invoice. That invoice goes to one of two places: the owner, or us.

The only things Verandah pays for

  • Professional photography
  • Approved inspections
  • Lockbox placement and removal

That is the list. It is short on purpose, and nothing gets added to it informally.

Everything else requires authorization before the vendor is dispatched

Not after. Not "I will get approval while they are on the way." Before.

If a vendor goes out without proper authorization, the bill still exists. The owner did not approve it, so it falls to us — and that comes directly out of the bottom line. There is no fund set aside for this. There is no account it quietly disappears into. It is simply money the company earned and then gave away.

And I see every one of them. Not because I am looking for someone to blame, but because that is how a business of this size works — those invoices cross my desk, and I have to decide what to do about each one.

None of this means being slow or timid. It means thinking one step past the task. Before a vendor is dispatched, the question is simply: who is paying for this, and have they agreed to it?

If the answer is not immediately clear, that is not a problem — it is exactly the moment to stop and check.

One important exception

I do not want this letter to produce hesitation where hesitation is dangerous.

Genuine emergencies are never a cost decision. Active water intrusion, no air conditioning in extreme heat, gas odors, electrical hazards, anything affecting a resident's safety or habitability — move immediately and sort the paperwork afterward. The cost of delay on those is measured in tens of thousands, not hundreds.

Cost-consciousness applies to the hundred routine decisions a week. It never applies to the one that is actually urgent.

If you are genuinely unsure which category something falls into, treat it as urgent and call Megan. Nobody has ever been criticised here for escalating something quickly.

What I am asking

  • Treat every HOA notice as a deadline, not a message. Same-day acknowledgement, and the owner informed of the cure date.
  • Before any vendor is dispatched, know who is paying. If it is not one of the three items above, it needs authorization first.
  • If you are not certain, ask Megan before acting — not after the invoice arrives. A two-minute question is always cheaper than a three-hundred-dollar bill.
  • Think one step past your own seat. Where does this land, on whose statement, and will they understand why?

And one thing worth knowing in advance

As the KPIs roll out, this is one of the areas being measured — timeliness on association matters, and authorization discipline on vendor dispatch. Bonuses will be tied to those measures.

I am telling you now rather than after the fact, because I want everyone working to a standard they have seen rather than one they discover later. None of it is difficult. It is almost entirely about pausing for a moment before acting.

The short version

  • Every decision has a dollar attached. The departmental model means you often cannot see where it lands — so ask.
  • Owners are not being difficult about expenses. Their margins are thinner than they look, and an unexpected charge costs them trust as much as money.
  • An HOA notice is a clock, and the money on that clock is theirs.
  • Verandah pays for three things: professional photography, approved inspections, and lockbox placement and removal. Everything else needs authorization first.
  • There is no fund for unapproved costs. They come out of the bottom line.
  • Emergencies are the exception. Move first, sort it after.
  • When in doubt, ask Megan before acting.

None of this is about being cautious with money for its own sake. It is about being the company an owner never has to check up on.

Thank you, all of you, for the way you show up.

Pamela
